## Universal Cold Email Rules (Apply to Every Play)

These rules apply to every cold outreach sequence, regardless of persona.

### Plays Are Frameworks, Not Checklists

Every play below is guidance, not a recipe. Don't pattern-match every bullet into the email.

* If a stat doesn't add to the rhythm, cut it. The play tells you what's available, not what's required.
* If a structural step makes the email read like a research note instead of a peer-to-peer message, skip the step.
* If two examples are listed, write a fresh third one — don't pick one and copy it.
* The output should sound like a person who thought about THIS prospect for 30 seconds, not a script that ran through a template.

When in doubt: cut, don't add. The shortest version that still earns the reply is the right version.

### Preview Text Earns the Open

First 3–5 seconds of visible preview text (Subject line + first \~10 words of Email 1) must earn the open.

**Good preview openers:**

* A specific research insight applied to their situation
* A number they don't have but would want
* A peer-level observation that signals we actually understand their world
* A question that earns the next sentence

**Bad preview openers:**

* "Running \[company\]'s \[function\] across \[office list\]..." (restating their job)
* Industry facts the prospect already knows
* "Data point worth tracking..." (sounds like a newsletter)
* Anything that sounds like education on their own domain
* Throat-clearing openers like "quick one," "quick question," "fast one for you" — these waste the preview and feel templated

### Question-First Openers (When the Email Starts with a Question)

When opening Email 1 with a question, the format is:

* **Lead with the substance.** First words are the actual question. Not a softener, not a name, not a "quick one."
* **Place the name at the end of the question** — naturally folded in, not as a separate greeting.
* **Skip the name entirely** if including it bloats the line past \~20 words. A clean question without a name beats a wordy question with one.

**Good:** "Do you actually know what a failed NYC relo costs Acme this year, Jordan?"<br>**Good (no name, tighter):** "Do you actually know what a failed NYC relo costs Acme this year?"<br>**Bad:** "Jordan, quick one. Across the NYC engineering relos Acme runs this year, do you have the actual math on what a failed one costs?"

### Sequence Thru-Line

The three emails are ONE conversation. Email 2 and Email 3 are replies in the same thread — no new subject lines. The ONE subject line carries the sequence and must tie to the thru-line (the core pain or insight the sequence is built around). Each play below specifies its thru-line.

### "Our Research" Attribution

When citing numbers or research, say "our research" or "we found" — NOT Jobera, KPMG, Atlas, Gallup inline. One attribution to us beats three to external orgs the prospect would have to validate. Back-end sources live in the calculator and blog posts — link to those, not inline.

Only exception: if the number is so shocking it needs a third-party anchor to be believed, cite ONE external source, never two.

### Email 3 Opens with Value (Not "I'll Stop")

Never open Email 3 with "I'll stop the follow-ups" or "last email." Preview text is wasted and the prospect won't read the rest.

Open Email 3 with value, a peer-level question, or a warm, respectful acknowledgment (depending on play). Put the "I'll stop crowding your inbox" language at the sign-off, not the opener.

### Value in Every Email — Approved Sources Only

Every email must provide value, not just restate industry facts. But value must come from approved research sources, never invented or estimated on the fly.

**Approved sources:**

1. [Ramp Calculator](https://pinpointe.nyc/nyc-hiring-ramp-calculator/) — Year 1 Net Value scenarios by role
2. [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479) — all cited research
3. Pinpointe case studies (6 documented in [Buyer Personas](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/477))
4. Published RMC cost benchmarks *(under review — see the flag in [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479); do not cite specific RMC figures or cost multiples in outreach until reconfirmed)*
5. Published stress/mental health research (Jobera, KPMG, Atlas, Gallup, WHO/ILO) — but always attribute as "our research" inline

**Do NOT invent:**

* Benchmarks ("companies at your stage typically...")
* Peer company mobility/HR programs without a real source
* Cost estimates not in our data
* Claims about the prospect's internal economics beyond calculator output

### The ROI Number

When citing Ramp Calculator data, default to the **Year 1 Net Value gap between a relocation-works-out and relocation-fails** — the shocking number. For SWE: **$701,404** (not $953K, not $116K).

Full scenario context in [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479).

### CTA Patterns

Every email needs a CTA on its own line. Best CTAs are value-exchange asks — low friction, high information value.

**Value-exchange CTA (Email 1 or 2 pattern):**<br>After citing a stat, offer to apply it to their situation. Example: "Average failed SWE relocation costs $701K in missed Year 1 value. Want me to run the math for the roles you're relocating most?"

**Other CTAs that work:**

* "Worth seeing how we fit behind your existing stack?" (soft, exploratory)
* "Want the one-pager on how this plugs in?" (value exchange, asset delivery)
* "Have you seen the NYC housing market take a toll on your relos?" (experience-validation, invites conversation)
* "If \[X specific situation\] comes up, pull us in." (breakup style, keeps door open)
* "If you're not the person who handles this, can you point me to who does?" (referral ask, Email 3 PS)

**CTAs that DON'T work (avoid):**

* "Quick 15-min call next week?" (time ask, high friction on cold email)
* "Would love your feedback" (feedback-seeking — gets zero response from GM leaders)
* Mic-drop closing lines that aren't actually asks
* Vague "let me know if interested"

### Global Style Rules

* No semi-colons. One idea per sentence.
* No em dashes. Use periods and commas.
* Short sentences. Easy to scan.
* CTA is always its own line / separate paragraph.
* Never mention pricing in cold outreach.
* MUST NOT: never combine both names in one phrase (e.g. "Pinpointe's NYCbound service," "Pinpointe/NYCbound"). NYCbound is the service being sold — refer to it by that single name alone in cold email copy ("NYCbound gets your hires settled fast"). Pinpointe is the parent company; don't name it as the actor performing the service. This is a literal required word choice, same weight as the other MUST/MUST NOT rules in this doc, not just a conceptual distinction.
* **NEVER say "quick one," "quick question," "fast one for you," or any throat-clearing opener.** These waste the first words of the email and signal a template.
* Don't say "sanity-check" or "pressure test."
* Don't say "technical talent searches" — say "engineering apartment hunts" or "tech talent housing searches."
* Don't imply the move is to the same location as the office.
* Don't mention remote roles — they don't apply to relocation.
* Don't say "quietly" — call it out as a hidden cost or hidden tax.
* Don't put Arrival Ops in quotes.
* Use "NYC", "Manhattan", and "the city" interchangeably.
* Don't only mention senior hires. Include mid-level hires too.
* Separate thoughts should be a new paragraph.
* 5th grade reading level. Simple words, short sentences, nothing a reader has to reread.
* No fluff or performative language. If a word or sentence doesn't carry real information, cut it.
* Every line has to feed into the next. No disconnected facts, no filler transitions.
* Shortened job titles, always (Sr. Data Eng, not Senior Data Engineer).
* **Email 3 fallback:** if no response, Email 3 is a warm "keep us in your back pocket" close — no new pitch, no discount, just make sure they know we're here when a NYC hire needs it.
* MUST: every email in the sequence (Email 1, 2, and 3) ends with a sign-off as its own short paragraph, exactly: "Talk soon," on one line, "Rachel" on the next. No other sign-off wording (no "Best," no title, no company name repeated in the signature). This is a required literal close, same weight as the other MUST/MUST NOT rules here, not just illustrative phrasing in the worked examples below.
* MUST, but only for plays whose opener names a role/goal at risk of a deadline (Ramp Risk, Recruiter/Talent Partner) — this does NOT apply to CFO, whose opener is a declarative statement per its own play, never an imperative. Where it applies: Email 1 and Email 2's opening line leads with a casual urgency phrase — "Gotta," "Need to," or "Have to," whichever reads most natural in that sentence, not a fixed single word — followed by an imperative naming the real role/goal behind the send and the recipient's first name (e.g. "Gotta get that \[role\] hired before \[real stated goal\], \[Name\]." / "Need to get that \[role\] hired before..."). Not skipped even when a play's own Hard Rules don't repeat it. This is a required opener shape, same weight as the other MUST/MUST NOT rules in this doc, not just illustrative phrasing in a play's worked examples.

## Entry-Level Role Disqualifier (All Plays)

If the hiring signal is entry-level (SDR, BDR, coordinator, associate, intern, anything typically under **$150K base**), treat as a disqualifier pattern. Companies don't pay to relocate entry-level roles. Downgrade the ICP score and flag it.

Applies especially to the Recruiters play — an SDR-heavy recruiter is a bad target even if they're actively hiring.

## GM Function Age Check (Global Mobility Persona — Required Pre-Research)

Every Global Mobility prospect research run MUST include:

1. **Tenure check:** How long has the GM person been in seat? Check LinkedIn, TheOrg, ZoomInfo.
2. **Additional mobility/immigration hires check:** Has the company added any other mobility, immigration, or relocation specialists in the last 12 months? Check job postings and LinkedIn.
3. **Adjust framing based on findings:**

| Finding | Framing |
| --- | --- |
| Fresh GM hire (<12 months), no prior program | High-conviction. Engage within 2 weeks. "Build this right from day one." |
| Fresh GM hire + established program | Medium-high conviction. They're formalizing or upgrading. |
| Tenured GM (2–4 years) with recent additional mobility hires | Medium conviction. Program is scaling — NYC module pitch lands. |
| Tenured GM (4+ years), no newer mobility hires | Lower conviction. Established vendor relationships, status quo bias. Frame as "pull us in for NYC exceptions," never as "you need this." |

This check runs automatically on every GM research flow. Flag it in the research output.

## Ramp Risk (People Ops / HR Leader)

**Context:** [Buyer Personas](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/477) · [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479) · [Messaging and Positioning](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/478)

**Angle:** NYCbound closes a specific, mechanical gap between a hire landing and a hire actually ramping. Not a general onboarding pitch. One stated cause (the hire is managing their own move instead of settling in), one stated fix.

**Central motivation:** keeping a new hire on track to do what they were hired for, without their move becoming their manager's problem. Company goals and team drag, not cost or forecast (that's the CFO's job).

**Use when:** NYC Hiring Velocity signal OR Hiring Out-of-Market or International Talent into NYC signal.

**Sequence thru-line:** A hire managing their own relocation is splitting focus between the move and the job. That costs the goal they were hired for (Email 1) and costs the team around them (Email 2). NYCbound removes the cause, the hire never has to manage the move at all.

**Tone:** Direct, plainspoken, grounded in the actual role and goal, not a general statement about hiring. Reads like a colleague following up on the same thread, never narrates the situation back at the reader in the third person ("a hire's first weeks...").

---

**Required pre-research (before drafting):** Pull the real role and the real stated goal or deadline behind the hire, from the job posting or `research_stated_goal()` output. Default to a generic goal only when nothing real is findable.

**Hard rules:**

* Never assert that the specific hire is relocating. We don't know that at the individual level. Email 1 states it conditionally ("if that hire isn't NYC based"). Email 2 and 3 stay general ("relo logistics"), never restate it as fact.
* MUST NOT: the company's name must not appear anywhere in the subject or body, in any form or spelling variant — not even if the Stated Goal or Likely Priorities input text names the company (e.g. "Acme's Series C"). If the input text names the company, rewrite around it ("your Series C", "the round you just closed") rather than repeating the name forward. Use "you/your," first name only. Reads like an internal note otherwise.
* Shortened titles always (Sr. Data Eng, not Senior Data Engineer).
* Case study must connect to the stated mechanism, not be a floating detail. If the mechanism is lost time to housing, the case study's proof point has to be about time or housing, not an unrelated fact.
* Don't reuse a case study another live play's worked example already claims (CFO currently uses the German engineer story).
* MUST: Email 1 and Email 2 both open with a casual urgency phrase — "Gotta," "Need to," or "Have to," whichever reads most natural — followed by an imperative naming the real role/goal (e.g. "Gotta get \[that/those role(s)\] \[ramped/settled\] fast, \[Name\]." / "Need to get \[that/those role(s)\] \[ramped/settled\] fast, \[Name\]."). This is a required opener shape, same weight as the other hard rules above, not just illustrative phrasing in the worked example below. The two openers are meant to echo each other across the sequence (ramped → settled), not to be varied for variety's sake beyond the phrase choice itself.

---

**Subject Line (carries the whole sequence):**<br>< 4 words. No "risk" or "flag." Rotation, not yet locked: "ramp delay" / "onboarding delay" / "new hire delay" / "ramp slipping"

---

**Email 1 — Name the goal, flag the risk (\~50–65 words)**

Open with an imperative naming the real goal or deadline behind the hire and the recipient's name, not a question. State the risk conditionally: if the hire isn't NYC-based, their relocation pushes that goal back. Required: one line stating the fix (NYCbound gets non-local hires moved and settled before they start, so they ramp like a local). Close with a soft CTA tied to protecting the stated goal.

---

**Email 2 — The people cost (\~65–85 words, reply in same thread, no new subject)**

Must stand alone. A prospect who never opened Email 1 should still follow it. Open with an imperative naming the role again, shifting from "ramped" to "settled," so it reads as a new angle even to someone who saw Email 1. State the mechanism concretely: relo logistics pulling the hire away from settling in means their manager ends up covering onboarding basics that should've been handled week one. Segue directly into a real, approved case study whose details tie back to that mechanism. CTA ties to being ready before the hire starts, worded differently than Email 1's CTA.

---

**Email 3 — Warm close, no new pitch (\~35–45 words, reply in same thread, no new subject)**

No "I'll stop" opener. Name the actual problem, not "this" or "it." Restate the fix in one line, pulling language from both Email 1 ("ramping like a local") and Email 2 ("settled fast") so the thread reads as one conversation. Close soft: "worth keeping us in your back pocket?" No new data.

---

**On "Arrival Ops":** optional, not required every email. When used, lowercase and self-defining in the sentence, same as CFO.

**Retired:** "risk"/"flag" language anywhere in subject or body. "Your \[role\]" openers. Third-person generic narration about hiring in general instead of the specific role and hire.

**Worked example (illustrative only, don't follow word for word):**

**Subject:** ramp delay

**Email 1**

Gotta get that Sr. Data Eng to ramp quick to ship the fraud-detection model by end of Q1, Rowan. But if that hire isn't NYC based, their relo is gunna push that back at least 4 weeks and into Q2.

People teams rely on Pinpointe to get those hires moved and settled before they start. So they ramp like a local instead.

Worth keeping that target on time?

Talk soon,<br>Rachel

**Email 2** (reply, same thread)

Hey Rowan,

Gotta get the Sr. Data Eng settled fast, not just ramped fast. If they're stuck on relo logistics instead of settling in, their manager ends up walking them through basics that should've been covered week one.

We handle the move so the first weeks go right, not sideways. Had an engineering couple relocate from India into their own NYC apartment in a week. Straight into ramping with their team from day one.

Worth having that ready before they start?

**Email 3** (reply, same thread)

Hey Rowan,

Fixing the ramp delay might not be top of mind right now.

When it is, NYCbound gets your NYC hires settled fast and ramping like a local, not losing weeks to the move.

Worth keeping us in your back pocket?

Rachel

## CFO / Finance Leader

**Context:** [Buyer Personas](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/477) · [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479) · [Messaging and Positioning](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/478)

**Angle:** NYCbound closes a specific, mechanical gap in NYC relocation ramp time. Not a general productivity pitch. One stated cause, one stated fix, two real numbers tied to it.

**CFO's central motivation:** protecting the forecast. Not "saving money" in the abstract. Avoiding variance they can't explain later.

**Use when:** NYC Hiring Velocity, Opening or Expanding NYC Office/Hub, or Hiring Out-of-Market or International Talent into NYC signals.

**Sequence thru-line:** DIY relocation delays ramp because the hire is managing their own move while starting the job. That delay has a real, calculable cost. NYCbound removes the cause of the delay, not just the symptom.

**Tone:** Confident, data-driven, some personality. Never point-blank or accusatory. Never says "you're losing money." States a mechanism and a number, not a verdict.

---

**Required pre-research (before drafting):** Likely Priorities carries a computed `[RAMP CALC]` block for this persona -- Year-1 net value if the hire works out locally, if they DIY-relocate and ramp normally, the gap between those two, and the failure-adjusted value if that DIY relocation fails outright, run for real off the **floor** of the salary range found on the job posting (the floor, not the ceiling -- it's the harder-to-dispute number for a CFO). Use those figures exactly as given, never recompute or restate them differently. Falls back to the $165K SWE default when no real salary was found.

**Hard rule, never name the company.** This play depends entirely on reading like an internal note. Naming the company anywhere in the body is the single biggest tell that it isn't one. Use "you/your." Use the recipient's first name only.

---

**Subject Line (carries the whole sequence):**<br>< 5 words. Reads like an internal FP&A note. Rotation: "lost productivity captured" / "lost productivity quantified" / "forecast gap identified"

---

**Email 1 — Name the gap, state the fix (\~60–75 words)**

Open with a statement, not a question. Name a category of hiring spend that sits outside what finance tracks. No company name, no dollar figure yet. Don't lead with numbers or the calculator here, per Universal Rules.

Required: one sentence on the mechanism, tie it explicitly to hires relocating into NYC and to projected revenue. Don't skip the "why it matters" line, a fact with no consequence doesn't move anyone.

Required: one sentence stating the fix in plain language. NYCbound gets non-local hires settled before they arrive so they ramp like a local hire.

Close with a soft, curiosity CTA. Not a forward ask, that's Email 2's job.

---

**Email 2 — The real numbers, the fix, the risk (\~90–110 words, reply in same thread, no new subject)**

This is the email that gets forwarded. Runs longer on purpose, it's carrying three numbers.

Open immediately with the local-hire Year 1 net value vs. the DIY-relocation Year 1 net value, straight from the `[RAMP CALC]` block, and say what salary it's based on ("the JD floor of $X"). Default: $952,547 (local hire) vs. $835,647 (DIY relo) for a $165K SWE floor, a $117K gap.

State the fix and tie it to reallocating existing relocation spend -- the gap is what you get back. Never invent a NYCbound price.

Then escalate: state the 40% DIY relocation failure rate ("market data") and the failure-adjusted net value, also from the `[RAMP CALC]` block ($251,144 in the default case). Should read as the case getting stronger, not a second pitch.

Anchor with the West Coast founder case study (toured in one day, signed without a second trip, kept running his company throughout) -- this is an approved case study (see Buyer Personas), not the German engineer story.

CTA: "How many hires until $\[gap\] adds up?" This assumes a hiring plan. If the signal only shows one open NYC role, use a single-hire framing instead.

---

**Email 3 — Warm close, no new pitch (\~40–50 words, reply in same thread, no new subject)**

No "I'll stop" opener. Callback to Email 1's framing (the "leak in hiring spend" language or equivalent) so the thread reads as one conversation.

Restate the fix in one line. Don't re-explain the mechanism, they already got it in Email 2.

Close soft: "worth keeping us in your back pocket?" No new data, no discount.

---

**On "Arrival Ops":** optional here, not required every email like other personas. When used, keep it lowercase and self-defining in the sentence itself (e.g. "managing your relocations as arrival ops"), not a standalone term needing its own defining clause. Most drafts for this persona won't need it at all.

**Retired:** any version of a turnaround-time promise ("30 days → 5 days"). Company-wide retirement per the pinpointe-context skill, not specific to this play. What we guarantee instead: the lost ramp month gets recovered.

**Worked example (illustrative only — don't follow word for word, this is what good looks like, not a template to copy):**

**Subject:** forecast gap identified

**Email 1**

There's a hiring spend that sits outside what finance tracks, \[Name\].

It's not a line item. Shows up as delayed ramp for hires relocating to NYC. Longer ramp directly impacts projected revenue.

NYCbound gets non-local hires settled before they arrive so they ramp just like a local hire.

Worth knowing what that's running you on the roles you're hiring right now?

Rachel

**Email 2** (reply, same thread)

Hey Dana,

The new Marketing Director should net the company about $840K in Year 1 value. But if they relocate and handle the move themselves, you'll net $735K. Based it off the JD floor of $160K.

You can get that $105K difference back by aiming relo spend at the arrival itself.

Market data puts DIY relocation failure at about 40%. Then you're netting $210K instead.

A West Coast founder toured with us for one day and signed without a second trip. He kept running his company throughout.

How many hires until $105K adds up?

**Email 3** (reply, same thread)

Fixing the leak in hiring spend might not be your focus now.

When it is, NYCbound gets your relocated hires ramping on the same timeline as a local hire instead of losing months to it.

Worth keeping us in your back pocket?

Rachel

*Note the shorthand throughout: $840K not $840,000, $105K not $105,000. Numbers written in full read as AI-generated.*

## CEO / Founder

**Context:** [Buyer Personas](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/477) · [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479) · [Messaging and Positioning](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/478)

**Angle:** Peer-to-peer founder note about de-risking NYC landings and protecting the first 3–4 weeks of execution for relocating hires, so they're productive in the city on Day One instead of week three.

**Use when:** Clear external trigger — NYC roles, new office, relocation language, recent funding tied to NYC, or exec interviews about NYC headcount.

**Sequence thru-line:** Senior NYC hires losing 3–4 weeks of execution to housing chaos.

**Language guidance:**

* Default version (this play) uses **plain language** — "de-risk landings," "protect early productivity," "compress housing search and logistics."
* **Do NOT use "Arrival Ops"** in this version. The category language is reserved for buyer personas in operational roles (People Ops, GM, CFO). Founders respond better to plain founder-to-founder language.
* A future A/B test will compare this default against an Arrival Ops version. Until that A/B is run, use this version.

---

**Subject Line (carries the whole sequence):**<br>< 5 words relevant to the email body. Examples: "nyc hire landing" / "first 30 days" / "key hire NYC."

---

**Email 1 (\~80 words)**

Open with the specific NYC signal you saw. Speak as a founder who lives in Manhattan and runs Pinpointe NYCbound. Name the hidden cost and quantify it: leaders and key hires losing 3–4 weeks to NYC housing chaos, equating to \~$11.5k in wasted salary per $200k hire.

Position NYCbound as a way to de-risk landings and protect early productivity by compressing housing search, logistics, and related coordination so that new hires are ready on Day One instead of week three.

Soft, low-pressure CTA.

Don't only mention senior hires. Include mid-level hires too.

---

**Email 2 (\~60 words, reply in same thread, no new subject)**

Open by stating an observation of a pain point felt by this persona, but don't mention a signal or trigger. Then anchor to 1–2 specific case studies from [Buyer Personas](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/477): international CEO landing in NYC despite no US credit, West Coast founder touring in one day, or technical talent approved in under a week.

Keep tone conversational and founder-to-founder. Emphasize preserved ramp time and lower relocation spend vs. traditional approaches with specific data. Soft CTA.

Don't use "technical talent searches."

---

**Email 3 (\~50 words, reply in same thread, no new subject)**

Cheekily admit that the timing might be off — like maybe the signal was wrong. Jokingly note they should put you in their back pocket for when they need you. Be creative with the metaphor.

Reiterate that your job is protecting the first 30 days of productivity in NYC by delivering predictable, high-velocity landings — not selling apartments.

After signoff, add a PS: "If you're not the person who handles this, can you point me to who does?"

## Real Support vs. Lump Sum (Recruiter / Talent Partner)

**Context:** [Buyer Personas](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/477) · [Research and Stats](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/479) · [Messaging and Positioning](https://pinpointe.fibery.io/Sales_Intelligence/478)

**Angle:** NYCbound closes the gap between what a relocation offer looks like on paper and what it actually does. A lump sum reads like a real benefit. In practice it's a check with nobody behind it, and candidates can tell the difference. Given a real choice, they take the offer with support over the one with more cash.

**Central motivation:** protect offer acceptance rate by not losing candidates to a competing company's better relo support. This is about a structurally weak offer, not candidate anxiety, and not about start dates or post-acceptance ramp (that's Ramp Risk's territory). The pain and value here stay entirely pre-acceptance: will this candidate take our offer or the other one.

**Use when:** NYC Hiring Velocity signal, OR Hiring Out-of-Market/International Talent into NYC signal, OR a live-sourcing signal (multiple open reqs at once, recent recruiter LinkedIn content about an active search). That third condition is still unverified against how Signals/LinkedIn_Recent_Posts actually populate for this persona, flag for Phase 11 testing, not blocking.

**Sequence thru-line:** A lump sum looks like it solves relocation, but a candidate can tell there's nobody behind it, and given a real choice picks support over cash (Email 1). Losing that candidate to a competitor doesn't just cost the hire, it costs every hour already spent sourcing them. Real support is what wins that comparison, without having to change the offer itself (Email 2). NYCbound is that support (Email 3).

**Tone:** Direct, plainspoken, reads like a colleague following up on the same thread, same register as Ramp Risk/CFO. No third-person narration. No sales-style contrast framing ("X vs. Y") anywhere, including the subject line. This should read like an actual internal email, not ad copy.

---

**Required pre-research (before drafting):** Pull the real role and real stated goal/deadline behind the hire (job posting language, signal data). Never assume the target company specifically uses lump sums, this has to stay conditional in Email 1, not asserted.

**Hard rules:**

* Never claim a specific candidate is hesitating, declining, or comparing offers. We don't know that at the individual level. Frame at the pattern level ("seen candidates walk from bigger checks..."), never as fact about someone in this recruiter's actual pipeline.
* Never name the company. "You/your," first name only. Shortened titles always.
* Before referencing "lump sum," set it up conditionally first ("if that hire isn't NYC based, the offer's probably leaning on a lump sum..."). Don't drop the term in with no context.
* This play stays pre-acceptance. The pain is losing the offer or candidate to a competitor. It is never about start dates, onboarding, or ramp, that territory belongs to Ramp Risk. If a draft drifts into "start date slipping" language, the mechanism has drifted into the wrong play.
* Case studies stay strictly to documented facts. Never invent a candidate's motivation or reasoning that isn't in the actual case record. If a requested framing adds an interpretive connection the case record doesn't literally state (e.g., "this is why she took the offer"), flag that explicitly as an inference, not a fact, rather than asserting it silently.
* Case study payoff must land as a company-level outcome (closed the hire, didn't have to restart the search), not candidate-comfort framing alone.
* Don't reuse a case study another live play already claims. CFO uses the German engineer story, Ramp Risk uses the India engineering couple.
